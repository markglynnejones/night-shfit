# Night Shift - Game Vision

## Elevator Pitch

Night Shift is a first-person cosy organisation game set in a fictional British physical-media retailer in the mid-2000s.

The shop is closed and empty after an enormous sale day. Almost everything is in the wrong place. Thousands of CDs, DVDs, games, box sets, and other physical-media products have been abandoned, mixed together, misplaced, and scattered throughout the store.

The player has one massive persistent objective:

> Put the entire shop back in order.

There are no repeating shifts or daily resets. The store is one enormous task that may take many play sessions to complete.

## Player Fantasy

The fantasy is not primarily "working in a shop".

It is:

> Give me an enormous mess and let me systematically fix it.

The satisfaction comes from:

- Discovering misplaced items
- Identifying what they are
- Learning where they belong
- Grouping duplicate copies together
- Filling empty shelf sections
- Clearing mixed piles of stock
- Creating order using the player's own workflow
- Learning the layout of the shop
- Learning the fictional media catalogue
- Looking back at areas that were once chaotic and are now immaculate

## Core Loop

1. Find a misplaced item.
2. Pick it up.
3. Inspect and identify it.
4. Work out the correct destination.
5. Carry it there.
6. Place it correctly.
7. Repeat until the store is restored.

The interaction should remain simple enough that the satisfaction comes from organisation, recognition, physical handling, and visible progress rather than mechanical complexity.

## One Persistent Task

The entire store is accessible as one long-running organisation project.

The player may save, quit, and return later with the world in the state they left it.

There are no:

- Repeating nights
- Daily resets
- Short mission loops
- Shift timers
- Clock-out objectives

Progress is the physical state of the shop itself.

## Starting State - Post-Sale Catastrophe

The game begins after the store's largest sale day of the year.

Approximately 80-90% of stock should require some form of organisation.

The shop should look overwhelming at first, but the disorder must feel believable.

Examples include:

- Shelves partially emptied
- Products returned to incorrect sections
- Items in the wrong alphabetical position
- Mixed piles on tables
- Cases abandoned on the floor
- Baskets containing unrelated media
- Duplicates scattered across the store
- Products left around checkout
- Stock piled behind counters
- Promotional displays disrupted
- Some unopened or unprocessed stock

A minority of items should remain correctly shelved so the player can infer what an organised section looks like.

## Player Freedom

The game should validate the finished organisation without forcing one method of working.

A player might:

- Finish one shelf at a time
- Work through an entire genre
- Gather every Rock CD into a temporary pile first
- Group duplicate albums before shelving
- Clear the floor before touching shelves
- Move between departments whenever they feel like it

The player's improvised organisation strategy is part of the experience.

## The Store

The setting is a fictional UK high-street media retailer inspired by the physical-media shops of roughly 2004-2008.

Potential departments include:

### Music

Likely the first and largest department implemented.

Possible categories include:

- Rock
- Indie
- Metal
- Pop
- Dance
- Hip-Hop / R&B
- Electronic
- Classical
- Soundtracks
- Compilations

Music is broadly organised by genre and artist, with duplicates grouped together.

### Film & TV

Primarily DVDs, including films, television box sets, comedy, children's media, music DVDs, and special editions.

### Games

Physical games for fictional era-appropriate platforms, organised primarily by platform and title.

### Miscellaneous Media

Potential later content may include singles, box sets, magazines, accessories, or novelty media products where they add to the store rather than creating a new management game.

## Fictional 2000s Pop Culture

The store contains an alternate fictional version of mid-2000s pop culture.

Examples might include recognisable affectionate parody such as:

- Blue Day
- Lincoln Gardens
- Tropical Apes

But not everything should be a direct word-swap joke. The catalogue should mix:

- Clear parody
- Indirect references
- Completely original fictional artists, films, and games

Players should sometimes wonder whether something is a reference or simply part of the fictional universe.

Do not copy real album covers, film artwork, game artwork, logos, or other protected assets directly.

## Catalogue and Physical Stock

The game distinguishes between a catalogue title and physical copies of that title.

Example:

`Blue Day - International Clever Person` is one catalogue definition.

The shop might contain six physical copies of it.

All six copies share the same catalogue information and artwork but have their own physical state and location.

This allows hundreds of unique media titles to produce thousands of physical objects.

## Target Scale

Exact final numbers remain flexible, but the finished game should aspire to the same sense of scale as other large organisation games.

A possible target is:

- 1,000-1,500 physical music items
- 800-1,000 film/TV items
- 300-500 game items
- 100-300 miscellaneous items

Potential total:

> 2,000-3,000+ physical items

These are long-term content targets, not immediate prototype requirements.

## Progression

Progress should primarily come from the player's growing mastery and the improving state of the shop.

The player naturally becomes faster because they learn:

- Artist names
- Cover artwork
- Genres
- Store layout
- Shelf locations
- Common duplicates

A new player may need to inspect every CD carefully. Later they may recognise a cover or spine immediately and know exactly where it belongs.

Traditional XP, levels, and skill trees are not required for the core game.

Organisation percentages or department milestones may be used as feedback, but should not replace the physical transformation of the store as the main reward.

## Atmosphere

The store should evoke a British media retailer around 2004-2008 through environmental details rather than exposition.

Potential elements include:

- Fluorescent lighting
- Worn retail carpet
- Metal shutters
- Chunky tills
- Old monitors
- Listening stations
- Security cases
- Promotional cardboard stands
- Sale signage
- Posters
- CD racks and display tables
- Rain against the shopfront
- Distant street noise
- Store PA announcements
- Original music inspired by the era

The desired reaction is:

> I remember shops feeling like this.

## What Night Shift Is Not

Night Shift is not:

- A shop-management simulator
- A customer-service simulator
- A business/economy game
- A series of timed shifts
- A survival game
- A narrative RPG
- A combat game

The organisation itself is the game.
